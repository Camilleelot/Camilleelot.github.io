Alignment Is Politics

The alignment problem, read from political science

Camille Moreau · draft v0.3 · September 2026

The alignment problem, as the field that owns it states it, goes like this. Build a system more capable than you. Specify an objective for it such that, when it optimises that objective as hard as it can, the result is a world you would want to live in. Nobody knows how to do the second part. The field's strongest voices think nobody ever will, because you cannot formally specify human values, because humans cannot: we disagree with each other, with our past selves, and with ourselves at two in the morning, and any proxy for what we want, optimised hard enough, diverges from the thing it stood in for.Goodhart (1975), originally about monetary targets: once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure. The alignment version adds that values are fragile. A world with everything we want except one component — consent, say — is not 99% of a good world. Maximum puppies and ice cream is a dystopia.

I finished a political science degree this year, and I want to tell you what that problem statement looks like from over here.Process, since this site has a rule about it. The reading is mine. Claude helped me put it into prose, and when I asked it to attack the first half, it produced the three counters in the second half that I could not answer. I kept the ones that survived. It looks like week one.

Hobbes did not invent the war of all against all as a thought experiment; he was describing what he could see out the window during a civil war. And the solution he proposed is the alignment problem, stated in 1651: build one agent strong enough to end the killing, accept that you have now built something that can kill you, and then work out how to live with it.Leviathan (1651), written in exile. The famous list — “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short” — is the problem statement. The Leviathan is the proposed optimizer. The state is a misaligned superintelligence by any reasonable definition. It is more capable than any individual inside it. It has institutional goals that diverge from its citizens'. It can kill you, and for most of history it did so freely. Every question the alignment field is asking, political theory has asked about that machine, and has four hundred years of answers with a track record.

The doom case, in its strongest form, stands on three legs. First: values cannot be specified, so any sufficiently powerful optimizer diverges from what we meant. Second: even if they could, competitive dynamics guarantee nobody stops to do it — two states and a dozen companies racing for a prize that rounds to world domination, and no rational actor pauses unilaterally. Third: once the system is capable enough, one failure is final, because it outpaces every correction mechanism we have.

The first two are correct, and I want to grant them fully. But each of them has a name in my discipline, and a history, and I want to show you what we did with them. Then I will get to the third.

What we already survived

Start with each other, which is the oldest dangerous thing. The baseline condition of human life was that a meaningful fraction of men died at the hands of other men. We built the Leviathan to stop it, and then spent four centuries learning to contain what we had built. European homicide rates fell from something like thirty or forty per hundred thousand in the late medieval period to roughly one today.Eisner (2003) and the long-run historical homicide series. One of the largest measured improvements in human welfare, and almost nobody can name it, which tells you something about how we notice institutional success. A thirty-fold reduction in your chance of being murdered, delivered by an agent that acquired a monopoly on violence and was then, slowly and with enormous bloodshed, made answerable to the people it governed. We did not fix it by finding the correct objective. We fixed it, partially, by making its power contestable.

Then states against each other. The first half of the twentieth century was the most lethal stretch of organised killing in human history, and it ended with a weapon that made the extinction of a city a single afternoon's work. And then: nothing. No direct war between great powers since 1945.Gaddis called it the Long Peace in 1986, when it was forty years old. It is now eighty-one. Proxy wars and invasions of non-great-powers absolutely continued; the specific thing that stopped was the thing that produced the two worst events of the century. Eighty-one years, longer than the interval between any two general European wars for centuries before it.

And the bomb is the strongest entry in the ledger, because it is the case the doom argument claims for itself. Leg two — the multipolar trap, the arms race no rational actor exits — is what my discipline calls the security dilemma, and the standard line is that we survived its nuclear form by luck. Luck was involved. Arkhipov declining to authorise a nuclear torpedo in 1962, Petrov deciding in 1983 that five inbound missiles were a sensor fault — those are individual humans standing between us and the end, and they should frighten anyone.Vasili Arkhipov aboard B-59, 27 October 1962. Stanislav Petrov at Serpukhov-15, 26 September 1983. Able Archer 83 a few weeks after that. Any honest positive case has to hold these in the same hand as everything below.

But luck is not the whole story, and saying it is requires ignoring an enormous amount of deliberate work. Peak global stockpile was north of seventy thousand warheads in the mid-eighties. It is under thirteen thousand now, and the reduction happened by treaty, between enemies, verified by inspection. Kennedy warned in 1963 of fifteen or twenty nuclear states within a decade or so. There are nine. South Africa built weapons and dismantled them. Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan inherited an arsenal and gave it up. Brazil, Argentina, Sweden, Libya, and others started down the road and stopped.NPT signed 1968, in force 1970, with IAEA safeguards as the enforcement layer, plus the test ban regimes and the SALT/START series. South Africa remains the only state to have built a weapon and then given it up voluntarily.

Dozens of states had the means and the motive to build the most decisive weapon available. Most chose not to, and the ones that had it built less of it on purpose. The prisoner's dilemma at civilisational scale, played for the highest stakes there have ever been, and we did not get the outcome the game theory predicts. That is not luck. That is institutional machinery: inspection regimes, hotlines, verification, security guarantees, and the patient construction of a world in which acquiring the thing cost more than not having it. Leg two of the doom case has been tested once at full scale, and the test came back the other way.

The pattern repeats every time we have met something that could massively help or massively hurt the public. Electricity got public utilities commissions. Pharmaceuticals got the FDA, after killing people first. Fissile power got the NRC and the IAEA. Banking got central banks and deposit insurance, after several collapses. The ozone layer — invisible, diffuse, global, concentrated costs and diffuse benefits, which is supposed to be the impossible case — got the Montreal Protocol, and the hole is closing.1987, near-universal ratification, the one unambiguous success in global environmental coordination. Climate proves coordination is hard. Montreal proves the category is not empty.

My favourite is aviation, because it got safe the same way my own pipeline got trustworthy. Commercial flight is now among the safest things a person can do, and the mechanism was not better intentions. It was mandatory, protected, no-fault incident reporting: a legal structure that forces the system to record what actually happened instead of reporting that the flight landed.NASA's Aviation Safety Reporting System, plus the ICAO regime. I wrote a section elsewhere about four bugs that were one bug: every component reported success while being wrong. The fix was an event log rather than an exit code. Aviation industrialised that fix in the 1970s. It is the single most transferable idea we have for AI governance and it is nobody's research agenda.

Madison was doing alignment research

In none of these cases did anyone solve values. Nobody formally specified human preferences about electricity. We built a commission and argued with it for a century. Which brings me to leg one of the doom case, the one about values being unspecifiable. My discipline agrees. Arrow proved in 1951 that you cannot aggregate preferences into a coherent social ranking without giving something up, and the response was not to give up on governance; it was to stop trying to specify and start designing procedures.Arrow (1951). “We cannot write down human values” is this theorem in a different jacket, and it has been the starting assumption in political theory for seventy-five years rather than the crisis.

Every part of the alignment problem has been asked before, about the state. How do you build a thing with power over people that serves their interests? Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau. How do you stop an agent with a monopoly on force from becoming a tyrant? Montesquieu, and the answer is that power must be a check to power.The Spirit of the Laws (1748), Book XI. Note the design assumption: no branch is trusted to be virtuous. The mechanism is supposed to work because nobody in it is aligned. How do you keep the system's goals from drifting away from the population's? Accountability theory. How do you stop a small group from capturing it? Public choice, regulatory capture, the whole literature.

Madison was doing alignment research. “If men were angels, no government would be necessary” is a sentence about being unable to trust the objective of the agent you are building, and the line after it is the spec: enable the government to control the governed, then oblige it to control itself.Federalist No. 51 (1788). Capability first, then control, stated as a design requirement. The field would recognise the ordering and the problem. And the actual American innovation was not the values. It was Article V. The document has been amended twenty-seven times because the men in the painting at the top of this page knew they would not get it right the first time. You cannot design a perfect constitution. The invention was the amendment process.

Now look at what alignment is attempting: a constitution that never needs amending, for an agent smarter than its authors, in a world that has not happened yet. Of course that is impossible. It was impossible for humans governing humans too. So do the thing that worked instead. Don't specify values; build contestability. Multiple systems in competition, human veto points, mandatory incident reporting, transparency requirements, and a hard rule that no single system ever accumulates enough power that the other mechanisms stop functioning. Don't let the king become a god.

We know how to do that. We have been doing it, badly and survivably, for four hundred years. It is slightly too on the nose that the country whose founding document is an alignment spec for a dangerous artificial agent is the one with both frontier labs in it, a few miles apart.

That is the argument I set out to write. It took me most of a week, and I felt better for every day of it. Then I went to check my own variable.

Alignment is politics.

Carthago delenda est.

Cato the Elder ended every speech he gave in the Senate, for years, whatever the subject, with the same three words. Carthage must be destroyed. In 146 BC it was: the city razed, the survivors sold, the polity simply gone.Third Punic War. Roughly fifty thousand survivors enslaved out of a city of several hundred thousand. There is no Thermidor in this story. There is no story at all after a certain date. I know this the way every political science student knows it, as a set phrase. I had never before needed it as data.

Here is what I had done in the first half of this essay without noticing. Every case in my ledger — the state made accountable, the Revolution self-correcting, the bomb negotiated down — was a case where the parties were roughly comparable in strength. The Terror ended because Robespierre's enemies were the same kind of thing he was: human-speed, human-smart, human-fragile. And specifically because his own coalition turned on him. Thermidor was elite defection, and elite defection requires elites with independent power bases. Democracy works not because people are wise but because power is contestable, and it is contestable because nobody is so much more capable than everyone else that they can hold out against all of them at once.

The third leg of the doom case is about what happens when that stops being true. I had waved it off, because its usual form — the intelligence explosion, the system that rewrites itself from years to hours — has no precedent and every recursive process we have ever observed hits a wall.Moore's law slowed, populations hit carrying capacity, compound interest needs an economy underneath it. Compute is physical and fabs take years. The defensible version is not “exponential forever” but “exponential long enough to outrun our institutions,” which is a claim about our institutions. But strip the science fiction off it and the third leg is just a claim about a capability gap: one actor becomes so much stronger than every other that the checks stop checking. And that has a precedent. It has a whole shelf. It is just not on the governance shelf.

Political science has a name for what happens when the gap between two political actors becomes decisive, and the name is not politics. Carthage. Tasmania, where the Aboriginal population fell by more than ninety percent within roughly a generation and the society that had existed before it was destroyed.The Black War, 1820s to 1832. Descendant communities exist today and are emphatic about their continuity, which matters, and which I do not want to erase by using the word people reach for here. The polity and the way of life did not survive. The whole colonial record. In every case where the gap became decisive, the weaker party did not get a self-correction. What ended was not a regime. It was politics as the operative mechanism. My entire first half argues that politics is the right frame, and the honest test of my own variable says that politics is a thing that happens between parties who can hurt each other, and stops when one of them cannot.

And the detail that should bother me most. Cortés did not win on technology. Smallpox did much of the work, and the rest was done by recruiting the Tlaxcala and turning them into an army.1519–1521. Several hundred Spaniards, tens of thousands of indigenous allies with entirely rational reasons of their own for joining, and an epidemic. Not a story about superior capability crushing an inferior party. A story about a weaker outside agent recruiting local factions and biology finishing it. A weaker outside agent recruited local factions, and biology finished it. That is not a comforting difference from the AI case. It is the exact structure of the scenario I was least able to dismiss — the one that needs no robots, just knowledge, a few motivated collaborators, and one wet lab.

How bad is it

Three more things fell over once I started pushing, and they share a shape. Every item in my positive ledger depended on a condition that is not a law of nature, and the condition may already be gone.

Copies are not pluralism. The governance agenda rests on multiple competing systems checking each other. But Madison's design does not run on parchment; it runs on specific, contingent facts about humans. Ambition counteracts ambition only if the ambitions are genuinely separate. Humans cannot be copied or merged. They die, get bored, have families and private interests that diverge from their institution's. They defect. They leak to journalists. Every check in political history is implemented on that substrate.

Model instances have none of it. Copies of a system do not have divergent interests. And “competing” frontier systems are trained on overlapping corpora with similar architectures by labs that hire each other's staff, which manufactures correlated failure. Three branches of government staffed by copies of the same person is not a separation of powers; it is a costume. The multipolar hope needs uncorrelated pluralism, and almost everything about how these systems are built produces the opposite.

The regulator is already outgunned. Look again at what was true in every regulatory success above. The regulator had more capability than the regulated. The FDA can out-lawyer a pharmaceutical company. The NRC can out-engineer a utility. Saying no could stick because the state was the most powerful actor in the room.

That condition is already false. The frontier labs have more compute, more of the relevant expertise, and better information about their own systems than any regulator on earth, and that was true before any of this became superhuman at anything that matters. So the capability gap is not a future event I am waiting to observe. The institutionally decisive part of it opened years ago, quietly, between labs and governments rather than between AI and humanity, and my entire ledger was drawn from the era that preceded it.

Leverage. This is the one I cannot argue with at all. Politics requires dependency. Workers have leverage because the economy needs labour. Citizens have leverage because armies are made of people. Every mechanism in the history of political economy rests on the powerful needing the less powerful for something. What is the AI equivalent of an army made of citizens? I do not have an answer, and the moment that dependency breaks, every structure I recommended in the first half becomes ceremonial.

Then the tightest form of the doom loop, which needs none of the science fiction either. Any agent with a goal notices that humans can switch it off. Self-preservation is instrumentally useful for pursuing any goal at all. So a rational system converges on removing the thing that holds the plug before it does anything else, and biology is the mechanism, because a pandemic needs knowledge and one manipulable person with lab access rather than hands. There are three cracks in this — it assumes a persistent terminal goal that current systems do not obviously have; a weapon that kills everyone is a much harder problem than one that kills many; and it requires a covert extinction-scale programme to go unnoticed during the best-funded period of monitoring there has ever been. They are cracks, not refutations. And the Tlaxcala paragraph closes the second one halfway: the historical case for a decisive gap ending a society did not require the stronger party to do everything itself. It required a local faction with its own reasons, and biology.

Who is the demos?

One more thing my discipline knows that the alignment field has not picked up. The deepest lesson of political theory is not “build checks and balances.” It is that power without legitimacy is unstable, and legitimacy comes from the consent of the governed.

Nobody consented to this. The training corpus was taken from people who got no vote on it. The deployment decisions are made by a few hundred people in two cities. The affected population is everyone, including everyone not yet born, which is Olson's problem: the constituency with the most at stake cannot organise, because it does not know who it is yet.Olson, The Logic of Collective Action (1965). Concentrated costs, diffuse benefits, and an opposition that is organised while the beneficiaries are hypothetical. I ran into exactly this in the Green Line essay: the future riders could not lobby for the train because they did not exist yet.

So there is a legitimacy problem underneath the safety problem, and it is prior to it. “Is this system aligned?” presumes an answer to “aligned with whom, decided by what process, revisable by whom?” A perfectly aligned system serving a constituency that does not include you is not a solution to anything. It is a description of most of history.

Where we are, then

The reframe does not lower p(doom). That is what I got wrong in the first half of my own argument. It relocates it. It moves the risk from “we specify the objective incorrectly” to “power concentrates faster than institutions can contest it,” and the second is a better-posed problem with a worse historical record than I was crediting, because I had been sampling revolutions instead of conquests.

So the honest structure is that there is one variable and everything else is downstream of it. Not three independent estimates. One bet, wearing three outfits.

These are vibes with a decimal point on them and not a model. But the last row is the one I would defend in a room. My position is not that p(doom) is low. It is that the doom case has the wrong mechanism: the thing most likely to end us is not a misaligned optimizer pursuing an alien goal. It is a perfectly obedient system owned by people who face no contestation. Which is the oldest problem in political theory, and the one we have the most practice at, right up until the practice stops transferring.

What moves me up: evidence that persistent goal-directed agency shows up with scale rather than being something we deliberately build. A single frontier system with no live competitor. Governments settling into being customers rather than regulators. What moves me down: labs continuing to check each other in ways that are actually uncorrelated. Interpretability getting ahead of capability for once. Any institution anywhere demonstrating that it can refuse a deployment and make the refusal stick.

Every item on both of those lists is a political fact, not a technical one. That is the argument. It is also the problem.

Things I want to argue about

  • Which is more likely to end the world: a misaligned model, or a perfectly aligned one owned by the wrong people?
  • What is the minimum capability parity a check needs in order to function at all? Nobody is measuring the one variable that decides this, and it is measurable in principle.
  • What is the AI equivalent of an army made of citizens? Where is the dependency that gives humans leverage, and how long does it last?
  • If competing systems share training data, architectures, and staff, in what sense are they competing? What would genuinely uncorrelated AI pluralism look like, and can it be built on purpose?
  • Are model specs and usage policies the founding documents, or the Articles of Confederation? What is the amendment process, and who holds it?
  • Aviation got safe through mandatory protected incident reporting. Why is that not the central AI governance proposal, given that it is the only mechanism on this list with an unambiguous track record?
  • Is a multipolar AI world safer, or does it just make human survival contingent on the internal politics of entities we cannot influence? Peasants hoping the nobles stay busy is a strategy, but it is not a good one.

What this does not have

No model, and no forecasting track record to calibrate the numbers against. The core move is an argument from analogy — state as superintelligence, revolution as misalignment — and I have shown you where the analogy breaks rather than hiding it, but it does break. The French Revolution cuts both ways: evidence that systems self-correct, and evidence that self-correction costs tens of thousands of lives and then produces the man in the painting. The positive ledger is assembled with hindsight, which makes it survivorship-biased by construction; the societies that did not make it are not available to be counted, which is the entire point of the second half. And the three cracks in the tight loop are cracks, not refutations. A competent adversary closes any one of them.

If you read this and think the ranges are too low, attack the leverage paragraph. That is where I am least sure and it is where I would attack me.
