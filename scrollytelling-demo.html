The Rezoning Paradox

How Calgary's blanket rezoning policy created unexpected housing market dynamics

Introduction

In April 2024, Calgary implemented one of North America's most ambitious rezoning initiatives, changing over 100,000 properties from single-family to multi-family zoning overnight. The effects were immediate and surprising.

The Baseline: Pre-Rezoning Market (2023)

Before the rezoning policy, Calgary's housing market operated under traditional single-family zoning rules. Development was limited, predictable, and slow. The average home price sat at $520,000, with modest year-over-year growth of 3.2%.

Single-family homes dominated the landscape, comprising 76% of all residential properties. Developers faced significant barriers to building multi-family units, requiring lengthy rezoning applications for each project.

April 2024: The Rezoning Shock

On April 15, 2024, Calgary City Council approved blanket rezoning for residential areas. Over 100,000 properties instantly became eligible for multi-family development. The real estate industry braced for impact.

Initial reactions were mixed. Developers celebrated the reduced red tape, while some homeowners worried about neighborhood character. Market analysts predicted a 15-20% increase in land values within six months.

The Paradox Emerges: Summer 2024

By July 2024, an unexpected pattern emerged. Rather than uniform price increases, the market showed dramatic bifurcation. Properties in inner-city neighborhoods saw land values spike by 45%, while suburban areas experienced stagnation or even slight declines.

This wasn't what economists predicted. The "paradox" was that blanket rezoning didn't create blanket opportunity—it amplified existing geographic advantages. Location became more important than ever.

Developer Response: Strategic Concentration

Development applications flooded city hall, but they weren't evenly distributed. Over 80% of new multi-family proposals targeted just 12% of the rezoned area— primarily transit-adjacent and walkable neighborhoods.

Developers made calculated bets. Why build a fourplex in the suburbs when you could build a 40-unit building downtown? The policy created winners and losers, not the level playing field advocates hoped for.

The New Equilibrium: Fall 2024

Six months post-rezoning, a new market equilibrium emerged. Inner-city land values stabilized at their elevated levels, while suburban properties returned to pre-rezoning prices. The total housing stock increased by 2,800 units—a significant jump, but concentrated in specific areas.

The paradox taught policymakers a crucial lesson: zoning reform is necessary but not sufficient. Without complementary infrastructure investment and transit expansion, blanket rezoning simply redirects development pressure rather than truly unlocking widespread housing supply.

Conclusions

The Calgary rezoning experiment demonstrates that housing policy operates within complex market dynamics. While blanket rezoning successfully removed regulatory barriers, it didn't eliminate the fundamental economic advantages of location, transit access, and existing infrastructure.

Future housing policy must recognize this paradox: regulatory reform is essential but insufficient. Cities need coordinated strategies that pair zoning liberalization with strategic infrastructure investment to truly unlock housing supply across all neighborhoods.
