The Rezoning Paradox

Why does every single person around me say housing sucks?

A finished housing development project, across the street from a stripped down single family home about to be torn down and developed. This area (5 minutes from my house) was rezoned a year ago, allowing duplexes and townhouses to be built in place of almost all single family homes.

The first question I think that’s on the mind of almost all people I know under 40 who aren't living with their parents is; What’s going on with housing? Why does housing feel like it costs so much? And most importantly from my perspective, a political policy lens, what's actually going on and can we as goverments and organizations do about it?

Intro; The What

The problem that can describe the vast majority of housing situations in the North American (or the vibe based feelings of gen Z) real estate market is the increasing lack of starter homes, affordable rent in high-employment and high-earning areas, which gates starting earners out of a geographical economic opportunity zone, as well as increasing pressures with an increasing population and a plateaued level of supply construction.

It would be a tall order to attempt to model the North American real estate market, but since this is not a unique problem to Calgary, Alberta, Canada, or even North America; I can use a recent set of decisions made within the last couple of years by the City Council of Calgary, responsible for zoning decisions on a scale of 1.4 million people (the population of Calgary), who have attempted to solve the lack of affordable housing models by rezoning large swaths of single-family home idings into duplexes, townhouses, and multi-family rental properties.

Calgary, although unique in that it has seen a relatively high increase in population, is stretching its infrastructure on multiple levels. Thus, city planners have had to make relatively radical decisions. Radical, according to the typical [North American, car-based, single-family home] city planning history spanning at least the last five decades. I have a paper about thisSee A Manufactured Crisis of Housing Affordability: A Community-Based Research Report of Affordable Housing in Calgary , and why moving away from car-based infrastructure is so hard, and how historical urban design cogs keep cities trapped.

The City of Calgary Blanket Rezoning Map of Calgary's Northwest The YIMBY theory suggests that increasing housing supply through rezoning, allowing development, and funding new construction, rather than controlling current housing, can alleviate concerns about housing. I want to investigate several things as a result of these decisions.

What questions should this project attempt to answer?

What changes were made, with what rationale, and what was the expected outcome?

Did anything change beyond a statistical significance?

If there was change, Did the policy achieve the intended effect on housing? To what degree?

If new housing has been constructed, is that housing within the class of affordable housing? did the trend of construction with regards to density change?

And a fun one, the main cited casualty of expanding housing supply through rezoning is the loss of community. Using the City of Calgary surveys, did this happen and to what degree? What correlation is there between the decisions made and the differences seen in the surveys over the years due to these decisions?

Results

Policy Changes and what they hope to accomplish

The changes seem much more simple, than implied, a 5 step program as alligned by the City of Calgary's Housing Strategy;

1. Rezoning the category of Residential to low and medium-density housing options.

2. Rezone to housing, to include a grade and spectrum to control density rulings.

3. Allowing a secondary suite and a backyard suite on the same property.

4. Removing a parking requirement for the backyard suite.

5. Adding a specialized Single-Detached Dwelling to code.

With the proposed reason for the change to be put quite simply,"Calgary needs more homes. Citywide rezoning will help increase supply."

The Real Analysis

What I actually found was paradoxical, using a ITS, total housing decreased by 23.9%. With apartment construction increasing by 27.1% and single family construction practically collapsed by 47%.

The pre-policy trend was 92.5 units/month, and housing growth was positive pre-change. Immediate impact was 642 units, as expected, but the post trend in policy was -74.7 units/month.

Test 1: Is this just seasonal variation?

Test 2: Was housing already declining? Considering the Pre-policy trend: 92.5 units/month, the trend significance: p = 0.0003. Gives us a conclusion: Housing was stable/growing before policy.

Test 3: Did uncertainty increase? Looking at volatility before: 385, volatility after: 415. The F-statistic: 1.16 (critical value: 3.01). Gives us the result that volatility remained similar.

How confident can we be in this data?

Let's use Cohen's d, to measure the effect size with pre/post change, and it amounts to 1.062, which gives us a Large effect.

Adding onto this a Bootstrap 95% confidence interval, with a policy effect of 409 units/month, 95% CI: [94,769], includes zero. No, the effect is statistically robust.

Rezoning Paradox Explained

1. With the data shown, and proven to be statistically important, what does this mean for what happened and what policy effect did this have? Well Single Family construction was absolutely devasted at -47% while multi-family increased at a rate of +27% which indicates a change in the types of construction but overall construction rates were a total net decrease.

2. The reality of time might be hitting expectations, developers need time to redisign projects, navigate new demands new markets new expectations and new approval projects, financing now looks different as now projects are both different in style, funding and market.

3. Market uncertainty, slightly increased volatility showcases that developers are still trying to understand what rezoning this large might mean before comitting to a new project.

Most importantly for me, this project showcased the need for more stringent data analysis, and challenging even seemingly intrisic ideas, the idea that making development easier, lessening restrictions led to less developments not more, was something that I would have never expected and I would have likely bet against.

I chose ITS over a simple before and after because it accounted for pre-existing trends and it gave us three parameters to test, baseline, immediate, trend change, which proved vital.

Beyond more abstract explanations, the reason for the paradox can be summed up in transition costs, developers abandoned projects and couldn't move fast enough to new ones which led up to a total loss.

Still, it's far too early to tell if the policy was a failure, likely post 24+ months and market stabilization could give proper data to see if this was a temporary or fundemental problem somewhere in the system.

My final words on this policy aspects of this project would be that support for the transitional period to developers would alieviate these concerns, and while rezoning is a free decision taken by the City of Calgary council, the changed imposed to the market require support to the developers, either through monetary support towards new denser constructions or and very importantly require time. And ultimately, this policy did lead to changes, that's why single family construction fell off a cliff and more denser homes rose up, but the scale of which was needed to cover the "More Homes for Calgary" solution wasn't supported as it should have been.

The Cost of Victory

In lieu of a lack of appropriate community feeling and police data, I'd like to offer a personal piece as someone who lives in a community with tons of development, these photos are all within five minutes of my house. This analysis actually explains some of the changes I have been seing in my community, empty lots, torn single family homes which have been not-developed for a while, it's rather sad actually, family homes which have been torn down, families who took the money and moved onto greener pastures leaving at times a literal hole in the community. That's the human cost to the progress, lower rent prices, more dense buildings and more construction means good things, but that's mutually exclusive to families staying in the same homes as their childhood memories.

It leads to images like this one, where a handmade birdhouse, maybe made as a summer project by a parent and child, is left hanging in the ruins of their house. You can see where the house was ripped open, and where the birds were meant to nest when coming back from the winter. To say something is lost here is an understatement, and while the policy goals of lower rent, more afforadable housing, more housing construction are goals worth striving for. But human and community cost of these achievements shouldn't be forgotten.

To say nothing of the homes that leave this community, the people who made up a before thriving community may now have taken the real estate valued and cashed in, with no fault to them, it leaves a feeling of emptyness and without new people and new families coming by way of new townhouses and new constructions. It leaves literal holes.

What Went Wrong/Limitations

Initially this Project was meant to be a simple comparitive analysis, as read in my methodology, I wanted to take a city within province and compare it to Calgary which recently went through rezoning,I thought this would be a good way to counter confounding factors from provincial and federal levels.

I then did the research, and my comparison city (Edmonton) went through the same rezoning, which meant I couldn't compare just on this level. My initial questions were far too broad, and far too reaching, and I had to sum down and retract a lot of the research I wanted to do to focus down on the scale of home building pre-post change.

Using a linear analysis was easy and rather simple and presented a convenient story, one the allinged with my ideas, with a broader ideological goal, but, I wanted to investigate more and it felt too clean of a solution considering the scope of data and scope of results.

Community feelings based surveys were also not available through the City of Calgary, and neither were crimes and disturbances distributed based on neigbhourhood so I couldn't use pre-existing official data to accurately provide data on community aspects.

Despite coming up on a year to the change, the time frame available isn't nearly there for a hollistic analysis of changes.
