§ 01 · Flagship · Long-form · Draft v0.9

The train was cut and restored. The vote didn't notice.

$1.5 billion in Green Line LRT funding, withdrawn and re-committed inside one electoral cycle. I went looking for the political signal. There wasn't one.

In 2025 I matched 225 federal ridings to Statistics Canada journey-to-work data across four elections. At the national level, the correlation is r = −0.11, p = 0.33. Nothing. I tested Calgary's 2018 BRT and Montreal's 2023 REM as natural experiments. Also nothing. I almost stopped.

Then I remembered the Green Line. A $6.2B LRT promised, cut, resurrected, and re-funded, all in one electoral cycle. One month after the Liberals re-committed $1.5B to Shepard Station, Shepard swung Conservative by +7.6 points, more than double the non-Green-Line ridings. The train being given back didn't reward anyone. The train being taken away didn't punish anyone. Transit was electorally invisible in both directions.

The piece lays out three readings, boring federalism, the neoliberal subject, a missing constituency, and doesn't pick one. Tocqueville, Olson, Schumpeter, and Gramsci all get their turn. There's also a section called "What went wrong and how I fixed it" that's honest about the redistribution mess, the spatial joins, and the afternoon I said fine, area-weighted is good enough.

A decade of Calgary's federal vote, and the cut that didn't register
Conservative vote share · 2015 → 2025 · Green Line ridings vs. all of Calgary
Two lines. One is the average across all of Calgary. The other is just the five Green Line ridings. They rise and fall in lockstep for ten years, through the cancellation, through the resurrection, through the election. Every swing is shared; the gap never moves.
The train was promised, killed, and resurrected on schedule. The two lines never separate. That's the finding.
§ 02 · Policy analysis · Nov 2025

The Rezoning Paradox.

Why did everyone around me keep saying housing sucked, after Calgary removed the rules?

In August 2024 Calgary went blanket-rezoning. I ran an interrupted time series on 17 months of CMHC data to test the YIMBY prediction. Starts fell −16% below the pre-existing trend. Composition did shift toward multi-family, but not enough to make up the gap. The paper walks through why deregulation alone, on a market mid-cycle, produces the counterintuitive result.

§ 03 · Applied research · Dec 2025 · Closer to Home

Borrowing a method from geology to find when retention changed.

Sequential Turning Point Detection, lifted from InSAR time-series analysis, applied to 70 months of nonprofit HR data.

I adapted STPD (Ghaderpour et al., 2024) to organizational data and used voluntary vs. involuntary turnover as treatment and control to rule out confounders. Three statistically significant turning points fell out. The Wellness Days policy (June 2023) corresponded with the largest positive shift in voluntary retention across the entire window, 3× larger than any preceding change. White paper to senior leadership, full Python implementation.

§ 04 · Tool · Python / Streamlit · Shipped

NGO Turnover Analysis Tool.

For HR managers who have never written code. Upload Excel, receive analysis.

I wrote it after watching an NGO's quarterly board report. "35% turnover" is a number. "48% of counselor turnover happens in months 3–12, costing $180K annually" is an argument. The tool categorizes every termination into six tenure windows, draws Sankey flows of department → role → outcome, and costs it out at C ≈ 0.8 × salary.
